# Field Notes: Pacific Lamprey
### *Entosphenus tridentatus*
*Compiled from USDA NRCS Biology Technical Note No. 51, "Pacific Lamprey and NRCS" (March 2011)*

---

## Quick ID

- **Not a fish in the usual sense:** a primitive, jawless, cartilaginous fish in the subfamily Petromyzontinae — eel-shaped, but with no paired fins, no scales, and no jaw
- **Mouth:** a round, sucker-like "oral disc" ringed with teeth — three large cusps plus posterior teeth
- **Gills:** multiple open slits along the side of the head instead of a single gill cover
- **Movement:** no swim bladder, so lamprey can't hover neutrally in the water — they swim with a snakelike, undulating motion or hold fast to a surface, constantly working just to stay in place
- **Adult size:** roughly 14–28 in returning from the ocean; can reach up to ~27 in at peak ocean growth

## An Ancient Lineage, an Understudied Life

Pacific lamprey have been part of Pacific Northwest ecosystems for hundreds of millions of years — but because they're not a sport or commercial fish, they've drawn far less scientific attention than salmon and steelhead sharing the same rivers. Much of their biology, especially the ocean phase, remains genuinely uncertain even to researchers today.

## Life Cycle — Seven Stages, Three-Plus Habitats

```
egg → larva (ammocoete, 3-8 yrs in freshwater silt)
  → macropthalmia (transforms: grows eyes + teeth)
  → migrates to ocean → adult parasite at sea (1-4 yrs)
  → returns to freshwater (~1 yr to mature)
  → spawns in a redd → dies (within ~3 weeks)
```

- **Redds:** dug by mating pairs moving individual stones with their mouths — about 1.5–2 sq ft, built in gravel/cobble tail-outs of pools, generally 7 in–3.5 ft deep
- **Fecundity:** highly variable — a female can lay anywhere from **20,000 to 200,000 eggs**
- **Hatching:** ~19 days at 59°F; larvae stay in the redd another week to a month
- **The larval stretch is the long part of the story:** newly emerged larvae (ammocoetes) are barely 0.3–0.4 in and drift downstream into silty, low-velocity pockets, where they burrow into U-shaped tunnels and **filter-feed on algae, diatoms, and detritus for 3 to 8 years** — several times longer than the time salmon spend as freshwater juveniles
- **Metamorphosis:** once ammocoetes reach 4–6 in, they transform into macropthalmia over several months — developing eyes and teeth for the first time as they leave the substrate and head downstream to the ocean

## Life at Sea — Parasite, Not Predator-to-Death

Adult Pacific lamprey spend just **1–4 years** in salt water (only about a quarter of their total lifespan) as external parasites, attaching to marine fish and mammals with their oral disc to feed. Notably, there's no evidence they seriously harm their hosts. They've been caught 300–2,600 ft deep and up to 62 miles offshore.

## The Return — Migrating at Night, Fasting the Whole Way

- Adults re-enter freshwater mostly **April–June**, sometimes as late as September for long hauls up systems like the Snake River
- **Almost all migration happens at night**
- Once in fresh water, adults **stop feeding entirely**, surviving purely on stored lipid reserves through a months-long wait — they typically go dormant over winter under boulders before spawning the following spring
- **Spawning window:** March–July depending on latitude and elevation; triggered around water temps of 50–60°F
- Unlike salmon, there's **no solid evidence lamprey "home" to their natal stream** — where they end up spawning is still an open question, though ammocoete pheromones may play a role in guiding adults to suitable streams

## An Unusual Way of Climbing

Lamprey can scale obstacles other fish can't — their oral disc works like a suction cup, letting them attach to a surface, rest, and advance in short bursts up through high-velocity water. But this only works on smooth, non-porous surfaces: fish ladders and culverts engineered for salmon (sharp corners, diffuser gratings, thin bar screens) often trap or block lamprey instead of helping them, since their body shape and swimming style are fundamentally different from a salmonid's.

## Field Sketch Notes

*The life-cycle wheel diagram in the source document (redd → egg → ammocoete → macropthalmia → ocean parasite → freshwater return → spawn → death) would translate well as a circular illustration — each stage in a genuinely different habitat, which is part of why the species is so vulnerable to disruption at any single point in the chain.*

## Conservation Status

Pacific lamprey have declined sharply across their range (Japan/Aleutians to Baja California), most severely in the upper Columbia, Snake, and North Umpqua basins. As of the source document:

| Jurisdiction | Status |
|---|---|
| Oregon | Sensitive |
| Washington | Priority |
| Idaho | Endangered |
| California | No special status |
| Alaska | Commercial |
| Federal | Species of Concern |

Primary threats mirror salmon's — dams and culverts blocking migration, water diversions, dredging, streambed disturbance, and reduced flows — but lamprey are hit even harder by infrastructure built with only salmon in mind, since fish-passage designs that work for salmonids often fail lamprey outright. They're also culturally significant, harvested for millennia by Yurok, Karuk, Nez Perce, Yakama, Umatilla, and other Northwest Tribes, who now co-lead restoration planning through the Columbia River Basin tribal restoration plan.
