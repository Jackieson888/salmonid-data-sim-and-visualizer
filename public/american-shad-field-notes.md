# Field Notes: American Shad
### *Alosa sapidissima* (Wilson, 1811)
*Compiled from USFWS "Freshwater Fish of America" series, Sept. 2023*

---

## Quick ID

- **Size:** females typically ~24 in, males ~20 in; max recorded length 29.9 in, max weight **12 lb 2 oz**
- **Family note:** a herring, not a trout or salmon — the largest member of the herring family found along the Atlantic coast
- **Feeding style:** a filter feeder — swims with mouth open, straining plankton out of the water with specialized gill rakers (called **ram suspension-feeding**)

## Range

- **Native range:** Newfoundland and Nova Scotia south to central Florida, historically spawning in nearly every suitable Atlantic coast river
- **Introduced range:** transplanted to the Pacific coast, where they're now established from Cook Inlet, Alaska down to Baja California, Mexico

## Habitat & Annual Rhythm

Adults spend most of the year at sea, shuttling between:
- **Summer feeding grounds:** Gulf of Maine
- **Wintering grounds:** further south, mid-Atlantic

Outside of spawning, they school near the surface over continental shelf waters and are also found in coastal brackish water. Feeding **stops entirely** once they turn into rivers to spawn — much like Chinook salmon, they run the spawning migration on stored reserves.

## Spawning — Timed by Temperature

- **Trigger:** peak spawning happens around **65.3°F**, typically between sundown and midnight
- **Timing varies by latitude:** adults arrive as early as November in Florida, but not until May–June in northern rivers
- **Method — broadcast spawning:** several males and one female swim tightly together and release eggs and milt simultaneously into open water — no nest, no redd
- **Output:** a single female can lay up to **600,000 eggs** in one season (helped by the eggs being fairly small)
- Shad are repeat spawners within a season — but *whether they survive it* splits geographically:

| Region | After spawning |
|---|---|
| South of North Carolina | Die after their one spawning season |
| Northern rivers | Migrate back to the ocean and can return in future years |

## Early Life

Juveniles stay in their natal river for several months post-hatch, then migrate to the ocean once they reach **2.8–4 inches** — in late summer in southern rivers, early fall further north.

## Field Sketch Notes

*A spawning aggregation would make a striking illustration: several silvery males flanking one female mid-water-column, everything happening in open water at dusk — no redd, no gravel, just a synchronized release into the current.*

## Conservation

American shad — alongside alewife and blueback herring — once anchored some of the **largest commercial and recreational fisheries on the Atlantic coast**. That collapsed under the combined weight of dams blocking migration, habitat loss, and overfishing, leading to the **closure of the at-sea fishery in 2005**.

Recovery efforts under the Anadromous Fish Conservation Act include:
- Active stocking in rivers across Massachusetts, Connecticut, Pennsylvania, Maryland, Virginia, and the Carolinas
- Work with dam owners to remove or bypass migration barriers
- Restoration of spawning and nursery habitat in rivers and wetlands
- Where dams can't be removed (often due to hydroelectric licensing), USFWS and NOAA prescribe required fish-passage measures — and since those federal licenses run **30–50 years**, each relicensing window is a rare, high-stakes chance to improve passage

Some populations are recovering, but fishing remains closed or tightly limited in many states.
