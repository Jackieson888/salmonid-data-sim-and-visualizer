# Field Notes: Chinook Salmon
### *Oncorhynchus tshawytscha* (Walbaum, 1792)
*Compiled from Alaska Dept. of Fish & Game Wildlife Notebook Series*

*Range-wide biology retained; Alaska-only harvest figures, drainage lists and
the Yukon migration case study were trimmed — this dataset is Lower Granite
Dam on the Snake River, not Alaska.*

---

## Quick ID

- **Also known as:** king, quinnat, tyee, tule, blackmouth (called "spring salmon" in British Columbia)
- **Diagnostic marks:** irregular black spotting across the back, dorsal fin, and *both* lobes of the tail fin — plus a black gumline, the source of the "blackmouth" nickname
- **In salt water:** deep-bodied, bluish-green back fading to silver sides, white belly
- **Spawning colors:** shift to red, copper, or deep gray — males run redder, females more gray
- **Sexing mature adults:** males (age 4–7) develop a hump-backed "ridgeback" profile and a hooked upper jaw; females stay torpedo-shaped with blunt noses
- **Juvenile tell:** fry show parr marks split by the lateral line; once they smolt for the ocean, sides go bright silver and the parr marks fade upward, mostly above the line

## Size — Notably Variable

Because Chinook can mature anywhere from **age 2 to age 7**, a single spawning run might contain fish ranging from a 4-lb three-year-old to a 50+ lb seven-year-old in the same gravel bed. Early-maturing small males that return after just one winter at sea are called **"jacks."**

**Record fish:**
| Record | Weight | Location/Year |
|---|---|---|
| Largest ever recorded | 126 lb | Fish trap, Petersburg, 1949 |
| Largest sport-caught | 97 lb | Kenai River, 1986 |

## Range

Stretches the full Pacific Rim: **California's Monterey Bay to the Chukchi Sea** on the North American side, and **Siberia's Anadyr River to Hokkaido, Japan** on the Asian side — including the Columbia and Snake river system this project counts.

## Life History Timeline

```
egg → alevin → fry → parr (1 yr in river) → smolt (migrates to sea)
  → 1–5 yrs feeding in ocean → returns to natal stream → spawns → dies
```

- Each female digs several **redds** (gravel nests) in fast, deep water, laying **3,000–14,000 eggs** total
- Eggs hatch late winter/early spring; alevins stay hidden in the gravel living off their yolk sac until they emerge as fry
- Juvenile freshwater residence varies by population: some hold for a full year before smolting (a "stream-type" life history), others migrate to sea within their first few months ("ocean-type") — the Snake and Columbia basins carry both, which is part of why this project's Chinook counts split into separate spring, summer and fall runs rather than one pulse
- Diet shifts with life stage: plankton → insects (freshwater juveniles) → herring, pilchard, sandlance, squid, crustaceans (ocean adults) — growth is fast enough that fish can **double their body weight in a single ocean summer**
- Adults stop feeding entirely once they re-enter fresh water to spawn, running on stored reserves for the whole migration — condition visibly deteriorates as the trip goes on
